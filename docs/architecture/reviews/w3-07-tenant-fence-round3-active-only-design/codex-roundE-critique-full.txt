Blocker #4 is directionally closed.

Blocker #1 is only partially closed because the proposed transactions establish an admission linearization point; they do not literally eliminate every gap between admission and the external effect.

The design can still become approvable by defining that distinction precisely and resolving the outstanding-capability and post-purge cases.

1. Transactional admission

For Textract and Bedrock, the two-item transaction is a valid fenced admission mechanism:

- quota mutation and `TenantLifecycleRecord.status = ACTIVE` succeed atomically;
- an operation whose transaction commits before `ACTIVE → DELETING` is admitted;
- one whose transaction is ordered after that transition fails;
- the quota OCC/retry behavior can remain intact.

But “there is no window” is technically false.

The process can pause, be descheduled, or lose its network connection after the transaction commits and before the provider call.

Deletion can linearize during that interval.

What the mechanism proves is not “the tenant is ACTIVE at provider-call time.”

It proves “this provider operation was admitted while ACTIVE.”

That is sound only if W3-07 explicitly adopts this concurrency contract:

> `ACTIVE → DELETING` blocks new admissions; operations already atomically admitted before the transition may finish.

Under that contract, Textract/Bedrock are closed architecturally.

The same distinction applies to Step Functions.

A transactionally created `ExtractionRun` is valid admission, but `StartExecution` may occur after deletion has linearized.

That is acceptable only as completion of a pre-DELETING admission.

There is also an important retry wrinkle in the current code: `startExecution()` runs even when `putIfAbsent()` reports an existing run.

The replacement design must permit a retry to recover an already-admitted run without requiring a fresh ACTIVE admission—or persist enough admission evidence to distinguish it from an unadmitted legacy/existing run.

Otherwise deletion between run creation and retry can either orphan a legitimately admitted run or accidentally restart one without proof of admission.

S3 presigning is not closed by “transaction, then next line.”

The presigned URL is a durable bearer capability.

A client may use it minutes after the tenant becomes DELETING, independently of the process that issued it.

Therefore the transactional slot creation proves that URL issuance was admitted while ACTIVE, but it does not provide immediate blocking of later upload use.

This needs an explicit policy:

- outstanding upload capabilities issued before deletion may remain usable until their short expiry;
- successful late uploads are quarantined/deleted and cannot become business state because confirmation/promotion is fenced;
- purge reverification covers objects arriving through still-valid URLs;
- the maximum URL lifetime is the documented residual-risk bound.

Without that statement, S3-presign remains a real exception rather than a closed instance of the synchronous pattern.

2. SES residual risk

The framing is honest, but the proposed plain read is weaker than necessary and does not yet satisfy the stated requirement closely enough.

“Bounded by worker latency” is also imprecise.

The relevant interval is not overall worker latency; it is the interval from the lifecycle read through recipient resolution, rendering, scheduling pauses, SDK retries, and the SES request.

There is no deterministic bound merely because the Lambda has a timeout.

The existing `PREPARED/FAILED_RETRYABLE → SUBMITTING` transaction is the natural admission point.

Add the ACTIVE `ConditionCheck` to that transaction.

Then SES gets the same explicit ordering rule:

- SUBMITTING claim before deletion: admitted send may complete;
- deletion before SUBMITTING claim: no send;
- retries after deletion cannot acquire a new sending admission.

This is stronger than a plain read, produces durable evidence, and avoids introducing a special consistency model for notifications.

A final plain read immediately before SES could be defense in depth, but it should not be the normative fence.

Privacy language should say “immediate blocking of new sends at the atomic send-admission point,” while explicitly allowing already-admitted provider calls to resolve.

If product/legal intent instead requires cancellation of every send not yet accepted by SES at the instant of deletion, neither the read nor the transaction suffices; a drain/lease protocol coordinated with the lifecycle transition would be required.

I do not think that stronger interpretation is necessary, but the document must choose explicitly.

3. `completeOcr`

The non-resurrection argument is sound for DynamoDB business-state persistence, provided every later persistence transition is genuinely fenced.

It is not, by itself, a complete LGPD/purge argument.

The OCR artifact in S3 is personal/business data even if no `ExtractedField` is ever created.

Calling it “not business state” must not make it invisible to deletion guarantees.

The resolution is acceptable only if all of these hold:

- the artifact key is deterministically discoverable from tenant/run ownership;
- late artifacts created after the first purge scan are found by reverification;
- versioned-object deletion handles every version/delete marker;
- the purge completion condition cannot pass while a late OCR writer can still create an undiscovered object;
- `SendTaskSuccess` cannot cause an unfenced downstream external effect;
- the artifact has a bounded TTL as another backstop.

The known `randomUUID()` artifact-key behavior makes this especially important.

If the purge inventory relies only on DynamoDB references that deletion may remove before the late `PutObject`, this design can leave an orphan containing OCR text.

Use a tenant-scoped deterministic prefix/key and make the final S3 rescan authoritative, or retain a durable inventory sufficient to find late objects.

Therefore: the chosen resolution is sound in principle, but blocker #3 contains conditions necessary to prove it.

4. Typed mutation lanes

This closes the architectural objection behind blocker #4, subject to concrete enforcement rules.

The good parts are:

- safe business mutation is the default;
- the executor appends the lifecycle check rather than trusting callers;
- exemptions are operation-specific;
- adding an exemption requires deliberate review;
- outbox metadata mutation is distinguished from creating tenant content.

The design document still needs to specify how raw access is prevented.

A branded TypeScript value alone is insufficient if ordinary modules can import an adapter exposing unrestricted `transactWrite`.

Require:

- raw DynamoDB transaction execution confined to persistence/infrastructure boundaries;
- business ports expose the fenced operation, not arbitrary transaction arrays;
- the brand/constructor is module-private or otherwise unforgeable without unsafe casts;
- boundary enforcement prevents feature modules from importing the raw executor;
- compile-time or architectural tests prove representative callers cannot choose `SystemMutation`;
- each system operation has a named reason and permitted lifecycle states.

Enumerate operations, not an asserted count of “three call sites.”

The lifecycle transitions, purge mutations, `tryAcquireLease`, and `markPublished` already imply more nuanced operations than that count suggests.

Also, allowing the outbox relay to publish after DELETING is safe only if every resulting consumer is independently fenced or deletion semantics intentionally require that event.

Bookkeeping exemption must not become an indirect external-effect exemption.
